Why the role exists

Most AI pilots die the same way. It is almost never the model.

MIT put the failure rate of enterprise AI pilots near 95 percent. The teams I meet have already tried a chatbot, a copilot, or a vendor demo that looked great in the sales call and went nowhere in the building. The gap is not intelligence. It is deployment.

  1. Built for the demo, not the mess
  2. Forced a migration nobody asked for
  3. Tuned for the happy path only
  4. Handed off to nobody after launch
  5. Never tied to a number

Method

One loop, run in order. Each stage gates the next. This is the part of the job that is judgment, not code.

  1. 1

    Discover

    The documented process is fiction. I sit next to the person doing the work, pull ten to twenty real completed cases, and walk the request back until I hit the actual bottleneck.

  2. 2

    Triage

    Every step gets one of three answers. A fixed rule becomes deterministic software. A messy judgment call goes to the model. Anything expensive to get wrong stays with a human.

  3. 3

    Build

    The smallest scope that proves value, on top of the ERP, CRM, or spreadsheet you already run. No migration. Your team's steps stay visible so trust survives.

  4. 4

    Evaluate

    An eval suite built from your own history. The pass rate is not the deliverable. The failure analysis is. Each failure becomes a fix, a guardrail, or a routing rule to a person.

  5. 5

    Deploy

    Shadow mode, then suggestions, then gated actions, then autonomy. Private inference, access control, and audit logs are in the build, not bolted on later.

  6. 6

    Prove

    Businesses measure three things: revenue, risk, cost. We define the number at scoping time and report the before and after in that language and no other.

  7. 7

    Scale

    One proven workflow exposes the next bottleneck up or down the line. The loop runs again. That is how a single deployment compounds across a business.

Engagements

Three shapes. Scope is directional and the success number is agreed before anything is built.

Two weeks

Discovery Sprint

I shadow the work, map the real process, classify your actual cases, and hand back a triage table with the rough math that says what a deployment is worth. You leave with a funded decision, not a deck.

  • Workflow map from the floor, not the wiki
  • Per-step call: software, model, or human
  • Baseline number and projected impact

Six to ten weeks

Deployment

The smallest build that moves the most work. Shipped inside your systems, with an eval suite built from your history and a rollout that earns trust one autonomy step at a time.

  • Working software in your environment
  • Eval report with failure analysis
  • Before and after in revenue, risk, or cost

Ongoing

Ownership

Production is cultural, not just technical. I stay on: monitoring, cost tracking, model changes, and the next workflow the first one exposed. There is no handoff to nobody.

  • Monitoring, evals, and model updates
  • Token spend and reliability tracking
  • Next bottleneck scoped and run

Best fit: a technical product meeting a non-technical team, real volume in the workflow, and an executive who owns the number. If the ask is “we're understaffed,” I will say so on the first call.

Deployments

Three systems I built and run. Each one replaced a process that lived in spreadsheets, inboxes, and one person's head. Two of them run inside organizations I am part of, so the numbers are mine to stand behind.

BCC Academy

Cohort program platform · Beyond Code Collective · bccacademy.io ↗

The problem

High-touch training programs for Black Girls Code, Upskill Bahamas, Catalyst, and After The Game were running on a spreadsheet, a Google Form, a Zoom account, and a part-time coordinator. Funders asked for outcome data nobody could produce.

What I built

One multi-tenant platform for enrollment, live sessions, attendance, certificates, and reporting. Zoom recordings import themselves. An Eventbrite ticket becomes an account and a magic link. New organizations are database rows, not deploys. An opt-in AI tutor runs per program, with row-level security, a PII audit log, and a weekly automated security check posted to Slack.

What moved

66/66

Every finished cohort to date completed at 100 percent. Five organizations live on one codebase, built in about nine weeks. It now also hosts the Forward Deploy training program for staff.

GFX1000

Design and video production pipeline · BGC and BCC marketing

The problem

Every design and video request was hand-built, and every review round meant three Slack messages per job. Reviewers were drowning and requesters had no idea where anything stood.

What I built

A request row in Notion comes out the other end as finished, delivered assets, with one human approval in the middle. A webhook crawler on Vercel catches the row and asks for missing fields in Slack, a Claude routine plans the work, a Cloud Run studio renders it, and delivery lands in Drive, Notion, Canva, and Slack. Nobody opens a terminal. The Forward Deploy channel publishes from the same system every morning.

What moved

136

Rendered videos across 35 registered projects, plus every still that came with them. One short published daily and a weekly analytics digest, unattended.

PulsePro

Project management for solo operators, plus an ad engine · pulsepro.work ↗

The problem

Freelancers and consultants with five clients were paying for tools designed for five hundred employees, and spending more time maintaining the tracker than doing client work. Their Shopify stores also needed fresh ad creative every week with nobody to make it.

What I built

PulsePro.work: projects, tasks, clients, billing through Polar, daily reminders over Telegram, and Claude-generated insights on the dashboard. Beside it, the Ad Engine: it reads a store's Shopify and Meta Ads data, has Claude write five Instagram video ad scripts a week, renders them with HeyGen, and hands back a performance report.

What moved

5 ads / week

End to end in under thirty minutes of machine time, at fifty to one hundred fifty dollars a month in marginal cost per client. The creative problem disappears from the owner's week.
